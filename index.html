How this works

Data sources, assumptions and limits

Data

  • Industry size, pay, output, turnover, occupation wages, posting duration and training requirements come from Lightcast's Q4 2026 data set for Boone, Kenton and Campbell counties combined (Kentucky Center for Statistics source data).
  • Your employees are split across occupations using your industry's own NKY staffing pattern (Lightcast, 6-digit NAICS rolled up). Industries with under 100 NKY jobs are blended with their broad sector's pattern.
  • Turnover is stabilized before it drives hiring. Lightcast's figure counts every separation, so it is reduced to the voluntary share using BLS JOLTS quits and separations rates by sector (August 2026; layoffs don't create the hires counted here). Small industries are blended toward their sector's rate, since one or two establishments can swing a small industry's year. Each occupation then uses its NKY turnover, scaled so the total matches. Temporary and seasonal hires you enter are set aside.
  • Net output lost per day a job is open is pay with benefits times your industry's NKY output per $1 of pay, minus the pay you don't spend while the job is open. Output per $1 of pay is kept between 1.1 and 3, and very small industries use their sector's figure.
  • Lost time is valued at what you pay for it, pay plus benefits. Each early exit or discharge costs recruiting, training time, half-speed ramp-up, and the days the job sits open again. For a typical NKY occupation that comes to about 30% of pay, in line with published estimates of 21% to 33%; occupations with long training cost more.
  • Weeks to full productivity come from each occupation's typical on-the-job training and education: 2 weeks with no training needed, 4 for short-term, 12 for moderate-term, and 26 for long-term or apprenticeship. Degree jobs get 8 to 12 weeks.
  • Recruiting cost per hire: SHRM. Trainer cost: BLS median training specialist wage of $33.31 (BLS) plus benefits at 30% of compensation (BLS). Intern conversion of 63%: NACE.

Limits to keep in mind

  • School engagement is modeled from the program's side. You already hire some of a program's graduates; engagement is assumed to bring in 20% more, and only those extra hires count as its benefit. The cost includes trainer time, coordination hours and materials. Educator data on program completions will replace the default program size.
  • Two cases are shown. The expected case uses the effects below. The conservative case halves every strategy effect, since workers who choose internships or training programs may already be more committed, and counts no state grant.
  • Later years are discounted at 5% a year, and training gains fade 20% a year (Almeida and Carneiro found 17% to 25%).
  • Upskilling gains follow the published rate: 10 hours of training per worker a year raises productivity about 0.6% (Almeida and Carneiro, Portuguese manufacturers), tapering as a worker's gap closes. A higher estimate exists: trained workers are 23% more productive in Konings and Vanormelingen, so this is the conservative choice.
  • Strategy effects are assumptions anchored to published retention gaps: graduates of a program your trainer committed time to are assumed to have half the career-fit exits of other hires and ramp up a third faster; intern hires 60% fewer (NACE finds more than three-quarters stay a year versus under half of other hires) and ramp up in half the time. The shape of each diminishing-returns curve is also an assumption. The NKY employer survey will replace these with local figures.
  • The three commitments are sized by rule: one school hour per employee a year, 2,000 internship hours per 40 employees, and 100 training hours per worker below proficiency. School engagement and internships go to the three jobs you hire most for; training covers every job.
  • Posting duration understates time to fill, because postings come down before a hire and jobs get reposted. You can raise it under Fine-tune.
  • Occupations marked "Limited NKY data" have under 250 NKY jobs or under 50 postings, so their defaults are less reliable.
  • The model runs year by year. Pipeline hires are assumed to separate at half the rate of other hires, so each year fewer jobs reopen and fewer replacements are needed. That is why savings grow over time. Upskilling gains are assumed to hold for the years shown.
